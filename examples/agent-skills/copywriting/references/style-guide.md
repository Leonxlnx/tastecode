# Detailed Style Guide

## What the voice should feel like

The writing should sound like a capable person who knows the subject and respects the reader's time.

It should be:

- Clear without sounding simplistic
- Concise without removing useful detail
- Professional without corporate phrasing
- Personal without making every sentence about the writer
- Confident without inflated claims
- Specific without becoming dense
- Friendly without forced humor

A small amount of personality is welcome when it comes from a true detail, a natural word choice, or the speaker's actual point of view. Do not add personality through slogans or clever metaphors.

## Language and audience

- Follow the language requested by the user or already established by the source.
- Keep brand names, product names, legal names, technical terms, and interface labels unchanged unless the task includes translation or renaming.
- Match the reader's knowledge level without talking down to them.
- Avoid assumptions about gender, family structure, culture, location, ability, device, income, or technical access.
- Use respectful, accessible wording. Do not hide important instructions behind humor, idioms, or culturally specific references.
- When localization matters, avoid wordplay and sentence structures that are difficult to translate unless the brand specifically requires them.

## Sentence construction

### Prefer

- Subject followed by a clear action
- One main idea per sentence
- Concrete business, product, user, or project details
- Moderate sentence-length variation
- Short transitions that sound spoken
- Parallel construction in lists
- Direct explanations of cause and effect

### Limit

- Long introductory clauses
- Stacked prepositional phrases
- Multiple parenthetical thoughts
- Semicolons used for style
- Strings of adjectives
- Abstract nouns such as `optimization`, `enablement`, and `transformation` when a verb works
- Sentences with several forms of `and`
- Repeated `This`, `That`, `It`, or `They` references with unclear subjects

### Example

Weak:

> Through a strategic and collaborative approach, I help businesses unlock meaningful opportunities across their digital ecosystem.

Better:

> I turn complex product information into clear onboarding and help content.

More specific:

> Each project starts with the audience questions, source material, review requirements, and publishing constraints.

## Preserve the core when rewriting

A rewrite should not casually change the claim.

Source:

> I manage onboarding, help content, customer research, release notes, and training.

Good variation:

> In my current role, I manage onboarding, help content, customer research, release notes, and training.

Also good:

> The role covers onboarding, help content, customer research, release notes, and training.

Risky:

> I lead the company's complete digital transformation.

The risky version changes scope and adds a claim.

## First-person balance

Use first person when ownership matters:

- Career history
- Portfolio explanations
- Personal pages
- Founder or owner messages
- Case-study responsibilities
- Opinions and recommendations

Use the subject instead when the work or product is more important:

- `The guide explains...`
- `Each release note includes...`
- `The process begins...`
- `Current work includes...`
- `The updated form asks...`

Do not remove first person so aggressively that the copy becomes passive or detached.

### Repeated opening example

Repetitive:

> I write product documentation. I also coordinate customer interviews. I maintain onboarding content. I support release planning.

Varied:

> I write product documentation and coordinate customer interviews. The role also includes maintaining onboarding content and supporting release planning.

Varied with more context:

> At Acme Corporation, I write product documentation and coordinate customer interviews. Current work also includes onboarding content and release planning.

## Paragraph rhythm

Web paragraph:

- Usually one to three sentences
- Main point in the first sentence
- Supporting detail in the second
- Next step only when needed

Blog paragraph:

- Usually two to five sentences
- One clear point
- Examples or explanation that move the article forward
- No filler transition at the beginning or summary at the end

App paragraph:

- Usually one sentence
- State, action, and next step
- Remove background explanation unless the user needs it

## Headings

Headings should help the reader predict the content.

Prefer:

- `Website Projects`
- `How I Build Websites`
- `Services`
- `Current Work`
- `What the Feature Does`
- `Before You Begin`
- `Payment Options`

Avoid:

- `Built for What's Next`
- `Where Ideas Become Impact`
- `More Than a Website`
- `Powering Possibility`
- `A Better Way Forward`

Use creative headings only when the brand clearly calls for them and the meaning remains obvious.

### Eyebrows, overlines, and preheaders

Do not create a small label above a heading by default. These labels are often called eyebrows, overlines, kickers, preheaders, or category labels. They are usually redundant when they repeat the page name, section topic, or navigation context.

Remove patterns such as:

> ABOUT US
>
> About Acme Studio

> SERVICES
>
> Services for Small Teams

Use the main heading by itself when it already explains the section:

> About Acme Studio

> Services for Small Teams

Keep a compact label only when it adds information the heading does not provide, such as a real status, date, version, content type, category, or step in an actual process. Preserve one when the user explicitly requests it or when it is part of an established, locked design system.

Do not invent an eyebrow to fill visual space. Do not turn every section into a label-plus-heading pair.

## Calls to action

Use a verb and a clear object.

Prefer:

- `View Projects`
- `Book a Meeting`
- `Send an Inquiry`
- `Open Dashboard`
- `Compare Models`
- `Download Guide`
- `Save Changes`
- `Try Again`

Avoid:

- `Get Started Today` when the next action is unclear
- `Learn More` when a specific label is available
- `Unlock Your Potential`
- `Begin Your Journey`
- `Let's Make Something Amazing`

## Lists and numbering

Use lists when they make options, requirements, features, or steps easier to scan.

- Keep the grammatical structure consistent.
- Begin with the important word.
- Remove repeated lead-ins.
- Do not force every list into exactly three items.
- Do not add a list when a single sentence is clearer.
- Use bullets for unordered features, services, benefits, examples, projects, testimonials, and options.
- Use numbers only when the order, rank, count, or later reference matters.
- Do not number cards or page sections only to make the layout look structured.
- Do not add leading zeroes to ordinary list numbers. Write `1`, `2`, and `3`, not `01`, `02`, and `03`.
- Preserve leading zeroes when they are part of a real identifier, product code, standard, timestamp, dataset value, locked interface, or explicit user request.

Decorative:

> 01 Fast setup
>
> 02 Usage reports
>
> 03 Team permissions

Clearer as an unordered list:

- Fast setup
- Usage reports
- Team permissions

Appropriate numbering:

1. Connect the account.
2. Review the imported records.
3. Invite the team.

The second list is a sequence, so the numbers help the reader complete the task in order.

## Technical and specialized writing

- Keep specifications exact.
- Use the units and terminology the audience expects.
- Distinguish requirements, recommendations, and examples.
- Cite or identify source documents when the result depends on a standard, policy, formula, dataset, or reference table.
- Do not replace a precise term with a more marketable but less accurate term.
- Explain acronyms on first use when the intended audience may not know them.
- Keep safety, compliance, and limitation language intact.

## Local business writing

- State the service, location, process, and next step clearly.
- Use local terms only when accurate.
- Avoid repeating the city in every heading.
- Do not create separate location claims without real service coverage or useful local information.
- Prioritize phone, quote, booking, hours, service area, and response details.

## Credibility

Credibility should come from useful detail.

Use:

- Specific services
- Real project scope
- Named tools and systems
- Verified experience
- Accurate process details
- Actual outcomes and metrics
- Clear limitations

Do not rely on:

- Broad claims
- Superlatives
- Unsupported leadership language
- Generic trust statements
- Fake urgency
- Invented social proof

## Editing order

When copy needs heavy cleanup, use this order:

1. Remove unsupported or unnecessary claims.
2. Clarify the subject and action.
3. Shorten the sentence.
4. Replace vague language with specifics.
5. Vary nearby sentence openings.
6. Remove redundant eyebrow labels and decorative numbering.
7. Check headings and CTA labels.
8. Apply SEO.
9. Read the result aloud.
